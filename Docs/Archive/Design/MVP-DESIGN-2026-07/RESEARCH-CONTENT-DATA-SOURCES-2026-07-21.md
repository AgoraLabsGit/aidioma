# Existing Structured & Processable Data for AIdioma Lessons — Survey and Licensing Report

**Date:** 2026-07-21 · **Scope:** ES↔EN data that can legally power a *commercial* Spanish-learning app (lessons authored as typed JSON: vocab items, translation sentence pairs with alternates + grammar tags + difficulty, aligned reading passages, conversation seeds; CEFR-sequenced; neutral Latin American Spanish, tú forms).

**Headline:** Yes — several sources are commercially usable and map cleanly onto individual schema atoms, but **no single source replaces original authoring**. The realistic win is a hybrid: Tatoeba (CC-BY) as a sentence-bank accelerant, Wiktionary/kaikki.org (CC-BY-SA, facts only) + wordfreq (CC-BY-SA data) as the vocab/morphology/frequency backbone, and public-domain FSI/government material as raw fodder for LLM rewriting — while treating the Instituto Cervantes PCIC, Anki decks, OpenSubtitles, and the Jehle verb DB as reference-only or off-limits.

## (a) Ranked shortlist

### 1. Tatoeba sentence corpus — the sentence-bank seed
**URL:** https://tatoeba.org/en/downloads · wiki: https://en.wiki.tatoeba.org/articles/show/faq
Community-curated sentences with cross-language links: ~443,900 Spanish and ~2,040,000 English sentences as of the current stats page (https://tatoeba.org/en/stats/sentences_by_language); the deduplicated, curated ES–EN pair export at ManyThings lists **144,215 Spanish–English pairs** (https://www.manythings.org/anki/). Text is **CC-BY 2.0 FR** — commercial use is explicitly fine with attribution ("sentences are from Tatoeba, with a link to https://tatoeba.org"); some sentences are additionally CC0 (https://en.wiki.tatoeba.org/articles/show/cc0-contributions). Format: TSV/CSV dumps with sentence IDs, language codes, links table (which gives you *multiple* English translations per Spanish sentence — a direct feed for your 3–6 accepted-alternates field). ~119,000 Spanish sentences have audio, **but audio is licensed per-contributor (CC-BY, CC-BY-SA, CC-BY-NC…)** — filter by license before commercial use. Quality/register: crowd-sourced, mixed peninsular/Latin American, uneven difficulty and occasional errors — so use it as a *candidate pool* filtered by your frequency list and LLM QA, not verbatim curriculum. **Verdict: ingest.** This is the single highest-leverage structured dataset for your sentence-pair atom and alternates generation.

### 2. Wiktionary via wiktextract / kaikki.org — the lexical + morphology backbone
**URL:** https://kaikki.org/dictionary/Spanish/index.html (raw data: https://kaikki.org/dictionary/rawdata.html)
Machine-readable JSONL of every Spanish entry in English Wiktionary — ~769K entries (incl. inflected-form entries), regenerated from fresh dumps (current file extracted 2026-07-20, ~966 MB). Fields per entry: word, POS, **gender**, senses/glosses (English), full **inflection/conjugation tables** (`forms`), IPA, usage labels (incl. "Latin America"/"Spain" tags), example sentences, translations. License: same as Wiktionary — **dual CC-BY-SA + GFDL** (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wiktionary). Commercial use OK, but **share-alike attaches to copied creative text**. Strategy: use it freely for *facts* (POS, gender, lemma↔form mappings, conjugation validation — facts aren't copyrightable), and avoid pasting its glosses/example sentences verbatim into your proprietary lesson JSON. **Verdict: ingest as validation/enrichment layer** — this is your conjugation database (better coverage than any dedicated open verb DB) and your POS/gender authority for the vocab atom.

### 3. wordfreq (Robyn Speer) — frequency ordering, done
**URL:** https://github.com/rspeer/wordfreq · README: https://github.com/rspeer/wordfreq/blob/master/README.md
Python package + embedded multi-source word-frequency data for Spanish (Wikipedia, OPUS OpenSubtitles 2018, SUBTLEX, NewsCrawl, Google Books Ngrams, OSCAR, Twitter, Reddit — cross-checked to damp outliers). Code **Apache-2.0**, data files **CC-BY-SA 4.0** — commercial use permitted with attribution; Speer obtained permission to redistribute the SUBTLEX-derived lists "for any purpose." Caveat: project is sunset — data is a ~2021 snapshot (https://github.com/rspeer/wordfreq/blob/master/SUNSET.md) — which is irrelevant for A1–B2 core vocabulary. Using it to *rank* your own vocab and grade sentence difficulty is fact-use; SA contamination risk to your lessons is effectively nil (don't republish the list itself except under SA). **Verdict: ingest.** Solves frequency ordering and powers a difficulty-scoring heuristic (rare-word density → your 1–5 difficulty field). Alternative with the same role: hermitdave's FrequencyWords (lists CC-BY-SA 3.0, from OpenSubtitles: https://github.com/hermitdave/FrequencyWords).

### 4. FSI Spanish Basic Course (+ other US-government courses) — public-domain raw material for LLM pipelines
**URLs:** https://www.livelingua.com/fsi/ · https://fsi-languages.yojik.eu/languages/FSI/fsi-spanish-basic.html · https://archive.org/details/Fsi-SpanishBasicCourse-StudentText
US Foreign Service Institute works = **public domain** (US government works; no license needed, commercial use unrestricted). Spanish Basic Course: 4 volumes, 55 units, thousands of ES/EN dialog lines, substitution/grammar drills (essentially proto sentence-pairs pre-tagged by grammar point!), recombination narratives, readings, plus hours of audio — explicitly **Latin American dialect** (set in fictional "Surlandia"). The catch: first printed **1957** — dated vocabulary and settings (telegrams, embassies), formal usted-heavy register vs your tú-neutral target, and source PDFs are scans (OCR cleanup or use the Archive.org OCR text as a starting point). Its *grammar sequencing* (Vol. 1: ser/estar, present tense, gender agreement → Vol. 2: past tenses, reflexives → subjunctive later) is a free, battle-tested syllabus skeleton that matches your grammar-tag taxonomy. **Verdict: ingest via LLM rewrite pipeline** — mine drills for grammar-tagged sentence-pair *patterns* and modernize wording; never ship text verbatim. Zero licensing risk, moderate-to-heavy processing cost. (Also check Peace Corps Spanish courses hosted at Live Lingua — likewise US-government materials, more recent and practical in register; verify the PD statement per course before ingesting.)

### 5. COERLL Spanish OER (UT Austin) — CC-BY conversation & listening seeds
**URL:** https://coerll.utexas.edu/coerll/materials/language/spanish/
A basket of resources with *per-project* licenses (verified on the materials page): **Spanish Proficiency Exercises — CC-BY** (hundreds of short videos of native speakers from across Latin America answering everyday prompts, *with transcripts* — ideal conversation-scenario seeds and authentic Latin American register models), **¡Listos! — CC-BY**, **Recorridos por la literatura hispana — CC-BY**, **Trayectos — CC-BY-SA** (full intro textbook), **Hecho en Texas — CC-BY-SA**. Avoid the NC ones: Spanish Grammar in Context, SpinTX video archive, Así me expreso (all CC-BY-NC-SA), Spanish Proficiency Training Website (CC-BY-NC-ND). No resource named "SpanDeck" or "Tarjetero" exists on the current COERLL Spanish page. **Verdict: ingest the CC-BY items** (attribution only, no SA) for conversation seeds and situational prompts; treat CC-BY-SA items as design reference or quarantined content.

### 6. Multilingual Central Repository (Spanish WordNet) — CC-BY sense network
**URL:** https://adimen.ehu.eus/web/MCR · OMW packaging: https://github.com/omwn/omw-data/blob/main/wns/mcr/LICENSE
Spanish wordnet synsets linked to Princeton WordNet 3.0. The Spanish component is **CC-BY 3.0** (English WordNet under the permissive WordNet license) — commercial OK. Maps to: synonym/alternate-translation generation (accepted-alternates field), semantic grouping of vocab into lesson themes, and distractor generation. Coverage is decent but glosses are sparse compared to Wiktionary. **Verdict: ingest as secondary enrichment** for alternates and theming.

### 7. Mozilla Common Voice (Spanish) — CC0 audio + sentences
**URL:** https://commonvoice.mozilla.org / dataset info: https://www.mozillafoundation.org/en/blog/common-voice-18-dataset-release/
Crowd-read speech clips tied to CC0 text prompts; entire dataset **CC0** — the only large *unencumbered* Spanish audio source (Tatoeba audio is license-mosaic; Forvo is paid). Heavy Latin American contributor base. Maps to: pronunciation audio for vocab/sentences, or training/eval data if you build speech features. Quality varies (volunteer mics); you'd curate. **Verdict: ingest selectively if/when you need audio**; otherwise TTS.

### 8. Tie — LibreLingo ES course (what it teaches about the gap) & ParaCrawl (scale reserve)
**LibreLingo-ES-from-EN** (https://github.com/LibreLingo/LibreLingo-ES-from-EN): course data in YAML under **CC-BY-SA 4.0**, but it's a *starter stub* — 11 commits, three modules (introduction/basics/activities). Confirms there is **no substantial open-source Duolingo-style Spanish course to import**; its YAML course schema is worth a look as prior art only. **ParaCrawl v9 en-es** (https://paracrawl.eu/): web-crawled parallel corpus under **CC0**, hundreds of millions of es-en pairs — legally frictionless but noisy machine-ish web text; useful only as a mining reserve for alternate-translation phrasing statistics, never as curriculum. **Verdict: reference / reserve.**

## (c) Bottom-line recommendation

**Keep "LLM-draft + human-review" as the production model — the data landscape changes the economics, not the strategy.** Nothing found is a drop-in lesson library. But three ingestion moves are clearly worth building:

**Ingest as seed data:**
1. **Tatoeba** → sentence-pair candidate bank. Pipeline: download dumps → filter es-en linked pairs → score with wordfreq rare-word density (difficulty 1–5) → LLM-tag grammar features (ser-estar, preterite, gender-agreement) → LLM-normalize register (LatAm/tú) → human review. The links table natively yields multiple accepted translations per sentence. Ship with a per-corpus attribution notice (CC-BY, no share-alike).
2. **kaikki.org Wiktionary JSONL** → the machinery behind vocab items: POS, gender, and full conjugation tables for *validating* every LLM-drafted vocab entry and every verb form in your sentences (replaces the NC-licensed Jehle DB; broader than UniMorph). Use facts, not verbatim glosses/examples, to keep SA away from your prose.
3. **wordfreq** → canonical frequency ordering for vocab sequencing and a difficulty signal. MCR WordNet (CC-BY) optionally for alternates/synonym expansion; Common Voice (CC0) when audio is needed.
4. **FSI Spanish Basic + COERLL CC-BY materials** → LLM-rewrite feedstock (see section e): FSI for grammar-sequenced drill patterns and syllabus order; COERLL Spanish Proficiency Exercises transcripts for authentic Latin American conversational seeds.

**Reference-only (never ingest text/data):** Instituto Cervantes **PCIC** — it is *the* authority for what belongs at each CEFR level for Spanish, and it's free to read at [cvc.cervantes.es](https://cvc.cervantes.es/ensenanza/biblioteca_ele/plan_curricular/), but it's a copyrighted book with no data license; use it (and the Routledge frequency dictionary, and Davies' lists) to *check* your level assignments, expressed in your own words/structures. No legitimate structured PCIC dataset exists; scraped GitHub copies would be infringing.

**Avoid for licensing reasons:** AnkiWeb shared decks (personal-use-only grant + laundered provenance), OPUS OpenSubtitles (NC + murky underlying rights), Fred Jehle verb DB (CC-BY-NC-SA), SUBTLEX-ESP/EsPal (research use), Radio Ambulante (NC + explicit prohibition), LibreTexts NC-licensed books, COERLL's NC-licensed projects, Kelly (no Spanish anyway).

**Does any single source change the "author everything original" plan?** Tatoeba comes closest: it can realistically supply 30–60% of raw sentence-pair candidates for A1–B1 after filtering, cutting drafting cost substantially — but every ingested sentence still needs the same human review as an LLM draft, and register normalization means many get rewritten anyway. So: same pipeline, cheaper inputs, plus free validation infrastructure (kaikki + wordfreq) that raises quality regardless of where sentences come from.

## (d) Licensing red flags to remember

1. **Share-alike (SA) contamination is the big one.** CC-BY-SA sources (Wiktionary/kaikki, UniMorph, wordfreq *data*, FrequencyWords, LibreLingo, Trayectos, Wikibooks) require derivatives to carry SA. If you paste their creative text (glosses, example sentences, lesson prose) into lesson JSON, that lesson content arguably becomes SA — a competitor could demand and reuse it. Mitigation: (a) extract only **facts** (POS, gender, forms, frequencies — facts aren't copyrightable); (b) keep any verbatim SA text in a quarantined, separately-licensed content bucket; (c) never mix SA text into your proprietary lesson corpus.
2. **NC kills it silently.** CC-BY-NC(-SA/-ND) blocks a commercial app outright: Jehle verbs, OpenSubtitles corpus, SpinTX, Spanish Grammar in Context, Kelly lists, Radio Ambulante, Merriam-Webster's *free* API tier. Check the exact CC variant on every COERLL/OER item — the same site mixes BY, BY-SA, BY-NC-SA, and BY-NC-ND.
3. **Attribution is per-source and sometimes per-sentence.** Tatoeba CC-BY requires visible credit + link; redistribution of their corpus expects per-sentence owner attribution (keep sentence IDs and contributor metadata in your DB from day one — retrofitting is painful).
4. **Audio ≠ text license.** Tatoeba text is CC-BY but each audio file has its own contributor license, many NC. Filter the audio metadata; don't assume.
5. **"Free" ≠ licensed.** Anki decks (default grant: personal study only, and uploaders routinely include copyrighted commercial content), scraped PCIC datasets, LingQ-style shared content — provenance you can't verify is liability you inherit.
6. **Public domain has edges.** VOA: only VOA-*produced* content is PD; embedded AFP/AP/Reuters material is not ([usagm.gov](https://www.usagm.gov/work-with-us/content-requests/voa/)). Project Gutenberg: text is PD but remove PG headers/trademark for commercial redistribution. FSI: the *original* government works are PD; third-party re-typeset/"enhanced" editions sold online may claim new rights — work from the Archive.org/yojik scans.
7. **GPL data (FreeDict) and AGPL code (LibreLingo)** are commercial-compatible in theory but create copyleft entanglement disproportionate to their value here — skip.

**Best processable bets:** FSI (for the drill→sentence-pattern mine and syllabus skeleton — accept that ~100% of its surface text gets LLM-rewritten, which is still far cheaper than inventing grammar-point coverage from nothing) and **COERLL Spanish Proficiency Exercises** (CC-BY, contemporary Latin American speech with transcripts — the single best register-matched seed for your conversation-scenario atom). Gutenberg readers and VOA are worthwhile secondary feeds for the reading-passage atom at A2+ and B1+ respectively.

**Commercial spend (MVP scale):** nothing is *necessary*. If budget exists, the two defensible buys are **Lexicala/K Dictionaries API** (https://lexicala.com/) for editorial-grade bilingual senses to QA vocab entries, and Davies' **wordfrequency.info Spanish lists** (~$20–40 one-time, https://www.wordfrequency.info/) as a second frequency opinion — both as internal QA tools rather than shipped content (check their redistribution terms; Davies' lists cannot be republished). Merriam-Webster's Spanish API requires negotiated commercial terms (https://dictionaryapi.com/info/frequently-asked-questions); Forvo-style audio APIs are unnecessary given Common Voice + TTS.
