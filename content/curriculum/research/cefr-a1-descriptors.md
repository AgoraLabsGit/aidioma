## 1. What A1 means

### 1.1 The global scale — A1 (paraphrased)
At A1 ("Breakthrough", the entry level of the **Basic User** band), a learner can:
- Understand and use **familiar everyday expressions and very basic phrases** aimed at meeting concrete needs.
- **Introduce themselves and others**, and ask/answer questions about personal details — where they live, people they know, things they have.
- Interact in a **simple way, provided the interlocutor speaks slowly and clearly and is willing to help**.

The defining A1 idea: **memorised, formulaic, personal, concrete.** The learner operates with a small stock of set phrases about themselves and their immediate world, is highly dependent on support, and cannot yet freely combine language.

### 1.2 A1 by skill (paraphrased summaries — text-relevant skills bolded)

**Reading comprehension (RECEPTION — core for translation input)**
- Overall: Understands very short, simple texts **one phrase at a time**, recognising familiar names, words and basic phrases, and **re-reading as needed**.
- Reading for orientation: Recognises familiar names, words and very basic phrases on simple everyday **notices**.
- Reading correspondence: Understands short, simple messages (e.g. on **postcards**).
- Reading for information/argument: Gets a rough idea of the content of simple informational material and short descriptions, **especially with visual support**.
- Reading instructions: Follows short, simple written directions (e.g. how to get from X to Y).

**Written production (PRODUCTION — core for typed translation output)**
- Overall: Writes **simple isolated phrases and sentences**.
- Creative writing: Writes simple phrases/sentences about themselves and imaginary people — where they live, what they do.

**Written interaction (INTERACTION — relevant if we add prompts/forms)**
- Overall: Can **ask for or pass on personal details in writing**.
- Notes/messages/forms: Writes numbers, dates, own name, nationality, address, age, date of birth — e.g. filling in a **hotel registration form**.
- Correspondence: Writes a short, simple **postcard**.

**Orthographic control (spelling — matters for graded typed answers)**
- Copies familiar words and short phrases (signs, instructions, names of everyday objects/shops, regularly-used set phrases); can spell their own address, nationality and personal details.

**Spoken production (out of scope now; relevant for later conversation modality)**
- Produces simple, mainly isolated phrases about people and places; can describe themselves, what they do, where they live.

**Spoken interaction (out of scope now)**
- Interacts simply but communication depends totally on repetition, rephrasing and repair; asks/answers simple questions on areas of immediate need; basic greetings, introductions, asking how people are.

**Underlying competences at A1 (calibration anchors)**
- **Grammatical accuracy:** only limited control of a few simple structures/patterns in a **memorised repertoire** (this is the ceiling — do NOT expect productive rule application at A1).
- **Vocabulary range:** a basic repertoire of **isolated words and phrases** tied to concrete situations.
- **Coherence:** links words/word-groups with the most basic connectors — essentially **"y" (and)** and **"entonces/luego" (then)**.

## 2. Curated A1 can-do statements for AIdioma's writing objectives (~20)

Paraphrased, reframed for a **translation + flashcard** app teaching **Spanish**. Each is written as an objective a lesson can target. (© CoE wording avoided; these are AIdioma's operational restatements.)

**Personal-identity core (the A1 backbone)**
1. Write your own name, nationality, age and address correctly (e.g. as if filling a form).
2. Produce a simple sentence introducing yourself: "Me llamo… / Soy de… / Tengo … años."
3. Write short set phrases to introduce another person (name, where they're from).
4. Write numbers and dates in words/figures (ages, prices, times, days).

**Everyday formulaic phrases**
5. Produce common greeting, leave-taking and courtesy formulas ("Hola", "Buenos días", "Adiós", "Gracias", "Por favor", "Lo siento").
6. Write a very short greeting message, e.g. a postcard-style holiday greeting.
7. Express basic thanks or an apology in one simple sentence.

**Concrete personal world (people, places, possessions)**
8. Write simple phrases stating where you live and describing your home in the most basic terms.
9. Write simple sentences about people you know (family members, relationships).
10. Write simple sentences about things you have (possessions), using "tener".
11. State simple likes/dislikes ("Me gusta… / No me gusta…").

**Basic descriptive sentences**
12. Describe a person, place or object in one or two very simple sentences.
13. Say what you do (routine/occupation) in a single simple sentence.
14. Indicate time with fixed phrases ("por la mañana", "el lunes", "a las tres", "en noviembre").

**Simple questions & answers (translation of both directions)**
15. Form and answer simple personal questions ("¿Cómo te llamas?", "¿Dónde vives?", "¿Qué tienes?").
16. Ask for something / offer something in a simple, direct way ("Quiero…", "¿Me das…?").
17. Ask and state quantity, price and cost in a simple transaction.

**Sentence-building mechanics (translation quality targets)**
18. Join two short ideas with a basic connector — "y" (and), and later "pero" (but) — staying within A1's linear, list-like linking.
19. Reproduce a small set of memorised sentence patterns accurately (word order, subject–verb, basic agreement) — accepting that control is limited and error-tolerant at A1.
20. Spell high-frequency A1 words with reasonable accuracy, especially words already in the learner's active/flashcard vocabulary.

**Reading-side objectives (feed the EN→ES prompt & flashcard cues)**
21. Recognise and understand familiar single words and very short phrases seen in isolation (flashcard recognition).
22. Understand a very short, simple sentence by reading it a phrase at a time, re-reading as needed (translation-prompt comprehension).

> **Design implications for grading:** At A1, expect (a) memorised/formulaic output, not free generation; (b) frequent, tolerable grammatical errors — the CEFR criterion is "usually clear what they are trying to say", not accuracy; (c) very short outputs (isolated phrases/single sentences), so translation items should be short; (d) heavy reliance on the personal/concrete domain — keep topics to self, family, home, possessions, numbers, everyday needs.

## 3. Pre-A1 vs A1 boundary (for calibrating Lesson 1)

**What Pre-A1 is.** The Companion Volume (2020) formalised **Pre-A1** as a band **"halfway to A1"** — a **milestone, not a full level**. It captures what a learner can do with a *very* limited repertoire before they meet the full A1 criteria. It is not a new bottom "level" with its own rich profile; many scales simply have a Pre-A1 rung, and roughly half of the CEFR scales have **no** Pre-A1 descriptor at all (some activities just can't happen that low).

**Core distinction:**
- **Pre-A1:** relies on a **small, memorised stock of words/expressions**, very heavy scaffolding (pictures, gesture, repetition, the interlocutor doing most of the work), and often single words rather than sentences. Tasks are frequently **purposeful classroom tasks whose primary focus is not language**. A large cohort is young/child learners.
- **A1:** can **combine** memorised material into **simple phrases and whole sentences**, ask AND answer simple personal questions, and produce short connected personal information — still formulaic, but sentence-level and two-way.

**Illustrative Pre-A1 can-do examples (⚠️ reconstructed from the CV; verify wording against official CV before publishing):**
- *Reading:* recognise familiar words/names accompanied by **pictures** (e.g. an illustrated menu, a picture book with familiar words).
- *Written production:* give **basic personal information** in writing (name, address, nationality), possibly with a dictionary.
- *Spoken production:* produce short phrases giving basic personal information (name, address, family, nationality).
- *Spoken/online interaction:* ask/answer questions about self and daily routines using **short formulaic expressions**, leaning on gestures/pictures.

**Calibration guidance for AIdioma Lesson 1:**
- If Lesson 1 asks the learner to **freely translate a full sentence**, that is already **solid A1**, not Pre-A1. To make an on-ramp, the earliest items should be **Pre-A1-flavoured**: single high-frequency words and 2–3 word set phrases (greetings, name, numbers) with strong support (flashcards, images, immediate model answers).
- A natural difficulty gradient: **word recognition (Pre-A1) → single set phrase → short full sentence (A1) → joining two ideas with "y"/"pero" (upper A1).**
- Because our app is text-first (no gesture/picture-heavy interaction), lean on **flashcard scaffolding and worked examples** as the Pre-A1 support substitute.

## 4. A2 headline — what changes from A1 (for the A2 outline wave)

A2 ("Waystage") is still **Basic User**, but it is a clear step up. Headline shifts (paraphrased):

- **Global:** understands sentences/frequent expressions in areas of immediate relevance (basic personal & family info, shopping, local geography, employment); communicates in **simple, routine tasks** requiring direct information exchange; describes their background/environment/immediate needs **in simple terms**.
- **From isolated phrases → linked sentences.** The signature A2 move in writing: learner can write **a series of simple phrases and sentences connected with basic connectors — "y" (and), "pero" (but), "porque" (because)**. (At A1 it was isolated phrases with barely "y/entonces".)
- **Writing scope widens:** short simple **notes and messages** on immediate needs; a **very simple personal letter** (e.g. thanking someone); short basic descriptions of events, past activities, personal experiences; simple accounts of family, living conditions, education, job.
- **Reading widens:** understands short simple texts on concrete familiar matters built from **high-frequency** (incl. shared international) vocabulary; finds **specific predictable information** in ads, menus, timetables, prospectuses; understands short simple personal letters and everyday signs.
- **New grammatical reach:** uses some **simple structures correctly** but still **systematically makes basic mistakes** (mixes tenses, forgets agreement) — crucially, A2 introduces **past-time reference** and comparison, which A1 lacks.
- **Interaction:** manages **short social exchanges** and simple routine transactions (shopping, directions, ordering a meal, using transport), though still can't sustain conversation unaided.
- **Topic domains expand** from purely self/immediate to shopping, local area, work/free time, travel, daily routines and simple past events.

**One-line contrast for lesson planning:** *A1 = memorised isolated phrases about me and my immediate world; A2 = simple connected sentences about routine matters, including simple past, joined with y/pero/porque.*

## 5. How CEFR relates to PCIC (Instituto Cervantes)

The **Plan Curricular del Instituto Cervantes (PCIC)** — *Niveles de referencia para el español* (published 2006) — is the Instituto Cervantes' Spanish-specific operationalisation of the CEFR. Where the CEFR is language-neutral and describes proficiency through holistic "can-do" descriptors, the PCIC takes the same six levels (A1–C2, grouped as A1–A2 / B1–B2 / C1–C2 volumes) and fills them with **concrete Spanish content**: twelve **inventories** per level band covering grammar (*gramática*), functions (*funciones*), notions (*nociones generales y específicas*), pronunciation/prosody, spelling, discourse genres and textual products (*géneros discursivos*), pragmatic-discursive tactics, cultural referents, sociocultural knowledge, and learning strategies. In short, **CEFR tells you what an A1 learner can do; PCIC tells you which specific Spanish grammar points, functions and vocabulary an A1 Spanish learner needs to do it** — making PCIC the more directly actionable reference for choosing AIdioma's actual Spanish lesson content, layered on top of the CEFR competence targets above. The freely browsable inventories at the Centro Virtual Cervantes (cvc.cervantes.es) are the recommended next research target for concrete A1–A2 Spanish grammar/function sequencing.
